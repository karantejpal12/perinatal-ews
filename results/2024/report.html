How well the forecast works

Tested forward in time: to predict each held-out year (2023, 2024), the model was trained only on earlier years. It is compared with two simpler methods. Higher AUROC and AUPRC are better; lower Brier is better.

Method used for this forecast: Gradient-boosted trees (perinatal-ews), chosen because it had the best held-out AUPRC.

95% bootstrap interval for the gradient-boosted model's AUPRC gain over the better baseline: 2023: -0.037 to +0.055; 2024: -0.047 to +0.034. An interval that includes zero means the ranking gain is not statistically clear.

County context (health, social, overdose and treatment-access data) was also tested as extra forecast inputs. Change in held-out AUPRC from adding it: -0.006 (95% CI -0.033 to +0.024). It did not clearly improve next-year prediction, so the forecast does not use it. County conditions describe where rates are high; they say little about which counties will change next year.

Unsupervised machine learning (k-means clustering) groups counties by their perinatal outcomes, size, adult health, social conditions, overdose burden and treatment access, giving each county a peer group. The number of types (4) was chosen by silhouette score; on 50 bootstrap resamples the grouping agreed with the full-data grouping with a median adjusted Rand index of 0.78.

How this works

  • Each county's expected 2024 rate comes from its own births in the 3 baseline years, skipping the year just before 2024 so a change that started then is not counted as normal.
  • Baselines for small counties are pulled toward the all-county rate (empirical Bayes), so a single noisy year does not set the bar.
  • Expected rates are scaled by the change seen across all counties, so a nationwide rise does not flag everyone at once.
  • Uncertainty includes ordinary binomial noise, uncertainty in the baseline, and the extra year-to-year variation measured in the data.
  • Alerts require a Benjamini–Hochberg q-value of 0.05 or less and a ratio of at least 1.10, and at least 20 events, the NCHS reliability threshold.
  • This is population surveillance to prompt a closer look by local teams. It is not a clinical tool and says nothing about individual patients.
